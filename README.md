**Get the 30 GB plan if** you're launching a personal blog, a portfolio, or a small business brochure site. 30 GB of SSD storage is genuinely hard to fill with a WordPress site — even a media-heavy blog with a few hundred images typically stays well under it. **Step up to 100 GB if** you're hosting multiple sites with real media libraries, running a small business site where you don't want to think about storage for a few years, or migrating in from a host where you've already accumulated a lot of content. The $4/month difference from the entry plan buys you 3.3x the storage and a 3 TB bandwidth ceiling.

**The 200 GB plan is for content-heavy operations** — think agencies managing client sites, or a site with a large download or media archive. If you're reading this and not sure whether you need 200 GB, you almost certainly don't yet, and the upgrade path exists.

One question worth asking: should you get shared hosting at all, or a VPS? RackNerd also sells KVM VPS plans starting at $1.83/month (1 core, 1 GB RAM, 20 GB SSD, billed annually), which is cheaper than the shared plans. But a VPS gives you an unmanaged server — you install and maintain everything yourself, from the web stack to security updates. Shared hosting with cPanel exists precisely so you don't have to do that. If you want to upload WordPress and start writing, shared is the right tool. If you want root access and enjoy server administration, that's a different conversation.

Here's the thing you won't find in a quick comparison table: **RackNerd does not offer a standard money-back guarantee**. Refunds are handled case by case rather than under a fixed 30-day policy, so you shouldn't order assuming you can change your mind risk-free. A third-party pricing tracker that verified all 49 of the company's plans in late October 2026 lists "none" in the refund column across the board.

The practical hedge is built into the pricing itself: shared hosting is **month-to-month with no contract**. Order one month of the 30 GB plan for $5.59, see how the site performs, and commit to longer terms only after you're satisfied. Annual, biennial, and triennial prepayment options exist in the order form, but there's no reason to prepay a host you haven't used yet.

The renewal side is less worrying. Those same verified plan listings show every plan renewing at the same price as signup — no jump from a teaser rate to a full-rate renewal, which is an annoying pattern with plenty of budget hosts. Your $5.59 plan stays $5.59.

One more cost note for completeness: EU customers may be charged VAT, and there's a 10% late fee and an $8 service transfer fee listed in the company's fee schedule. None of these should affect a normal, paid-on-time account.

## How to order (and the one mistake to avoid)

The ordering flow is short. These are the actual steps, per RackNerd's own order tutorial:

1. Open the shared hosting page and click "Order Now" on your chosen plan to enter the shopping cart.
2. Choose how to handle your domain: transfer it from another registrar, or use an existing domain and update nameservers later. Enter the domain and pick an extension (.com, .net, and so on).
3. Pick your billing cycle — Monthly, Annually, Biennially, or Triennially — and your **datacenter location**. The advice here is simple: choose the location closest to your visitors. LA for a North American audience, Singapore for Southeast Asia, Germany or France for Europe.
4. Enter billing details and pay. Payment methods include PayPal, credit card, cryptocurrency, and bank transfer; Alipay is also listed among accepted methods.
5. Accept the Terms of Service and complete the order. You'll get a confirmation email with login credentials and your invoice.

Activation is quick — accounts are typically up within **5–15 minutes** of ordering, and the company advertises instant activation on its shared hosting page. If it takes longer, support runs 24/7 with an advertised 10-minute average response time, reachable via ticket from the client area.

Now, the mistake to avoid: if the domain you enter is already hosted in your account, the order form may throw an error. The fix is to try a different extension or domain name to get through checkout, then sort the domain binding afterward. It's a small thing, but it's the most commonly documented hiccup in the whole process, and knowing it exists saves you a confused support ticket.
